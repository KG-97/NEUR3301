## What you need to know cold

### Development and heterogeneity

1. Precursors generate **neurons first, then glia**; within glia, **astrocytes are born before oligodendrocytes** — astrocytes are needed to establish the CNS.
2. Myelination continues **into the twenties** in humans, and different white-matter regions mature at different rates.
3. Astrocytes are hard to study: **no astrocyte-specific knockout mutants survive**, and astrocyte-specific transcription factors remain poorly defined. **GFAP** is the main marker, used in combination with others.
4. **Subtypes differ in function, not just shape:** radial, protoplasmic (grey matter), fibrous (white matter), Bergmann glia, velate, interlaminar. Neurons cultured on **striatal** versus **mesencephalic** astrocytes adopt different morphologies — regional astrocytes secrete different factors.

### Astrocytes and the synapse

5. **Neurons cultured without glia form poor synapses.** Adding astrocytes increases **mEPSC frequency and amplitude**, decreases transmission **failure rate**, and increases **synapse number**. Astrocytes also organise synapse **distribution** and, with microglia, **remove** synapses.
6. **The tripartite synapse** — astrocyte plus pre- and postsynaptic elements. Astrocytes express receptors for **most neurotransmitters**; transmitter release activates perisynaptic glia, raising intracellular Ca²⁺ and triggering **gliotransmitter** release.
7. **Gliotransmitters and factors to know:** glutamate, ATP, and **D-serine** (co-agonist required at **NMDA receptors**), plus **thrombospondins TSP1 and TSP2**.

### Astrocytes and the vasculature

8. **Blood–brain barrier.** Astrocyte **end feet** wrap the endothelial cells of capillaries. **Aquaporin-4** sits in the pericapillary end-foot processes and handles water and solute exchange; glucose transporters supply metabolic demand.
9. **Neurovascular coupling — the pathway to be able to draw:**

```text
Presynaptic glutamate release
-> astrocytic uptake -> rise in intracellular Ca2+
-> arachidonic acid (AA) mobilised
   -> via cyclooxygenase -> prostaglandins -> VASODILATION (more glucose and O2)
   -> via cytochrome P450 epoxygenase -> 20-HETE -> VASOCONSTRICTION
```

Metabotropic glutamate receptors participate. Nitric oxide also regulates vessel diameter and flow. Note the branch point: **one signal, two opposite vascular outcomes, decided by which enzyme handles arachidonic acid.**

### Astrocyte homeostatic functions

10. **Glutamate and GABA clearance.** Astrocyte-specific transporters **GLT-1 and GLAST** remove transmitter from the cleft; **glutamine synthetase** converts glutamate to glutamine for recycling back to neurons.
11. **K⁺ spatial buffering.** Neuronal activity raises extracellular K⁺; the local astrocytic membrane depolarises relative to distant membrane, and the resulting potential difference drives current flow that redistributes K⁺ through the **syncytial network** and out via end feet. K⁺ release at the end foot can also increase vessel diameter.
12. **Gap junctions and the syncytium.** Astrocytes occupy **non-overlapping territories** defining functional domains, coupled by gap junctions that pass molecules below a size limit. One astrocyte influences a large number of synapses — a **syncytial network** for rapid removal of build-up and delivery of what is needed.
13. **Extracellular space control.** Halving extracellular space **doubles** the concentration of anything toxic in it — so regulating ECS volume is itself a protective function.
14. **Other roles:** ensheathing and insulating unmyelinated axons, structural support, nutrient exchange, **iron regulation**, and production of growth factors and matrix molecules including proteoglycans. Also transient **lactate/ADP** supply during high activity (concept only — the diagram is not examinable).

### Microglia

15. **Origin is the classic exam point.** Microglia are **not** maintained from circulating monocytes. Fate-mapping shows they arise from **yolk-sac myeloid progenitors** that migrate in during embryogenesis **before the blood–brain barrier forms**, then persist as **self-renewing** populations independent of circulating macrophages.
16. Two brain macrophage populations: **microglia in the parenchyma** and **border-associated macrophages (BAMs)** in extraparenchymal compartments.
17. **Markers: ED1 and IBA1.** Microglia possess ion channels and receptors for signalling molecules, express MHC antigens, and transform from a **resting** to an **activated** morphology.
18. Activated microglia **proliferate, migrate to injury, recruit other cells, become phagocytic**, and can destroy inappropriate synapses, inappropriate connections and whole neurons, clearing debris.
19. **Synaptic pruning molecules to remember: TREM2**, and **CX3CR1 — the fractalkine receptor** — which supports bidirectional neuron–microglia signalling that maintains healthy neurons and flags damaged ones.
20. **Polarisation:** **M1 = pro-inflammatory**, **M2 = reparative/anti-inflammatory** (IL-10, TGF-β). Astrocytes have the parallel **A1 / A2** phenotypes. Subtypes exist; the cytokine lists do not need memorising.
21. Astrocyte pro-inflammatory output includes **IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α** and nitric oxide; **mTOR** is a named regulator.
22. **Crosstalk is bidirectional** — microglia can drive astrocytes into a reactive phenotype in culture, and there is evidence astrocytes influence microglial phenotype in return.
23. **TSP1/TSP2 increase** in reactive astrocytes and activated microglia after injury — but **decrease** in some disorders such as Down syndrome. The same molecule reads differently in different contexts.

### The glial scar

24. **Composition:** astrocytes and microglia, plus meningeal cells, fibroblasts and OPCs.
25. **Chondroitin sulphate proteoglycans (CSPGs)** are the inhibitory component — they do not support growth in vitro. **But** small amounts can be growth-promoting. Hodgetts flagged this both-sides point explicitly.
26. **Threshold behaviour:** below a certain level of response the two cell types negotiate the scar down; **above** that threshold the scar becomes **permanent** and inhibitory.
27. **Repair strategies:** **chondroitinase** to digest the scar — requiring controllable, switchable delivery (tetracycline-inducible viral systems) because too much degradation becomes its own problem; and **NeuroD1 reprogramming** of infiltrating astrocytes into neurons before they generate scar molecules. Both require fine control, since *some* astrocyte reactivity is necessary.

### Ageing

28. **Reactive gliosis is part of normal ageing** as well as injury. Learning-impaired aged rats show altered **proteoglycan** expression, notably in hippocampus, impaired diffusion, disorganised glial processes and uncontrolled gliosis.
29. **Glial decline → loss of homeostatic support**, which many take to define **cognitive ageing**. Whether over-active immune response causes ageing or results from it is genuinely unresolved — evidence points both ways.
30. Glial modulation is implicated in sleep, memory, pain, depression, memory loss and mood.

## The trade-off framing that runs through the lecture

Almost every astrocyte and microglial function has a protective face and a damaging face, and MCQs are built on forcing a single valence:

| Function | Protective | Damaging |
|---|---|---|
| Reactive astrocytes | Wall off damage, limit spread | Form growth-inhibitory scar |
| CSPGs | Small amounts can promote growth | Bulk deposition blocks regeneration |
| Microglial activation | Clears necrotic debris | Sustained M1 inflammation harms tissue |
| Gliosis | Normal ageing process | Uncontrolled, drives cognitive decline |
| Synapse removal | Refines circuits | Inappropriate loss of connections |

## Interrogation prompts

1. Retinal ganglion cells are cultured with and without astrocytes. Predict the difference in mEPSC frequency, amplitude, failure rate and synapse number — and state which of these is *not* explained by simply having more synapses.
2. Block astrocytic glutamate uptake. Predict the consequence for the synapse and for surrounding neurons, and name the transporters involved.
3. Knock out aquaporin-4 from astrocyte end feet. Predict the consequences for water handling and for K⁺ clearance.
4. A drug blocks cyclooxygenase in astrocytes but leaves cytochrome P450 epoxygenase intact. Predict the net vascular response to a burst of neuronal activity, and explain why the *same* calcium signal now produces the opposite outcome.
5. Extracellular space in a brain region is halved. What happens to the effective concentration of a toxic metabolite, and why is this an astrocyte problem?
6. Fate-mapping shows microglia are yolk-sac derived. Predict what happens to the microglial population if circulating monocytes are depleted in an uninjured adult, and explain why.
7. CX3CR1 is knocked out. Predict the effect on developmental synaptic pruning and on the microglial response to a damaged neuron.
8. Chondroitinase is delivered continuously and indefinitely after a spinal injury. Give the intended benefit and the predicted problem, and explain why an inducible system is used instead.
9. Why can NeuroD1 reprogramming of reactive astrocytes make the injury worse if it is too efficient?
10. TSP1 is elevated after injury but reduced in Down syndrome. What does this tell you about interpreting a single molecular marker as "good" or "bad"?
