## What the lecture is really about

Longer lives mean more people reach the ages at which neurological disorders become common. The lecture walks from **normal ageing** to **neurodegenerative disease** (AD, PD, HD), **immune-mediated demyelination** (MS), **vascular injury** (stroke) and **trauma**, and ends with **repair**. Four ideas carry across every section:

1. **Selectivity** — ageing and disease hit particular regions and cell types, not the brain uniformly.
2. **Thresholds** — large losses accumulate silently before symptoms appear (PD is the clearest case).
3. **Primary vs secondary damage** — the initial insult is followed by a cascade, and the cascade is the realistic treatment window.
4. **Symptomatic vs disease-modifying therapy, and the translation gap** — most approved treatments replace a lost output or blunt a cascade; many animal successes have not yet produced human efficacy.

## What you need to know cold

### Normal ageing

1. The brain shrinks and loses weight, mainly in cerebral cortex: narrower gyri, wider sulci, larger subarachnoid space.
2. Neuron loss is **region-selective**. Approximate retention after 60: occipital cortex ~50%, locus coeruleus ~60%, Purkinje cells ~75%, thalamus ~100%.
3. Cerebral blood flow falls (arteriosclerosis), raising stroke risk.
4. Dendritic branching simplifies, so synapse number falls. DNA methylation is presented as a contributor to synaptic change in ageing and age-related cognitive impairment.
5. Lipofuscin, tangles and plaques appear in normal ageing too, so their presence alone does not define disease. A figure shows the neurogenic niche shrinking with age.
6. **Glia and growth factors (figure data; species not stated):** GFAP rises with age in some regions (roughly doubled in entorhinal cortex, not significant in dentate gyrus). FGF-2 ligand stays stable, but receptors shift — neuronal FGFR1 falls ~25% in dentate gyrus while glial FGFR2 rises in entorhinal cortex. **A stable ligand does not guarantee stable signalling.**

### Exercise and trophic support

7. **Rodent:** voluntary running raises hippocampal BDNF mRNA and protein versus sedentary animals, and BDNF rises with distance run. Runners show more proliferating and surviving new dentate cells. The slide's inference: exercise boosts trophic support, neuron survival and neurogenesis, so more new cells integrate.
8. **Human:** hippocampal volume shrinks in late adulthood and BDNF declines with age. In the figures, serum BDNF and cardiorespiratory fitness each correlate positively with hippocampal volume.
9. **Walking study (labelled Erickson et al., 2011 on the slide):** over ~9 years, more walking predicted more grey matter in prefrontal, temporal and hippocampal regions, with the largest effect around 6–9 miles/week. BDNF mediation is offered only as *possible*.
10. The slide quotes ~1,000 new hippocampal neurons/day and ~2% annual turnover in humans. (Context beyond the slides: the extent of adult human hippocampal neurogenesis is still debated.)

### Alzheimer's disease

11. **Early-onset** (<65 years) vs **late-onset** (~90% of cases). APP and presenilin mutations are linked to early-onset; apoE is the third named gene. (Context beyond the slides: APOE ε4 is the main late-onset risk allele.)
12. Pathology: Aβ aggregates in senile plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, marked neuron and synapse loss, and **reduced acetylcholine**. The slide flags that plaque load does **not** necessarily track disease.
13. Progression figure: the slide labels the affected regions (hippocampus, where memories are first formed; language/word-finding; logic and planning; emotional control; sensory areas, where hallucinations can begin; and the sites holding the oldest memories) but does not number their order. (Context beyond the slides: pathology typically starts in medial temporal/hippocampal regions, and the oldest memories are among the last lost.) Sex differences in onset and progression are noted.
14. **Drugs relieve symptoms or slow progression only.** Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) slow ACh breakdown and are used early–moderate. **Memantine**, an NMDA-receptor antagonist, blunts excess glutamate — the same excitotoxic mechanism behind secondary damage after injury. Anti-amyloid antibodies: lecanemab, aducanumab.
15. A schematic Clinical Dementia Rating graph over 15 years ranks decline: untreated fastest, then inhibitor, then inhibitor plus anti-amyloid, then normal ageing. Its legend expands "ACEI" as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors although the slide is about cholinesterase inhibitors — confirm which is meant.
16. **Experimental directions:** mesenchymal stem cells reduce plaques and improve cognition in mouse AD, mainly via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant secretion; patient iPSCs (APP/PSEN1 mutations) model disease; spine plasticity as resilience; restoring low BDNF; APP solubilisation; microRNA-140 silencing.
17. **Non-drug approaches:** music, reminiscence, cognitive stimulation, spaced retrieval, exercise, diet; emerging rTMS, ultrasound, photobiomodulation and oxygen therapy.

### Parkinson's disease

18. Motor signs — tremor, rigidity, akinesia, postural instability — follow **reduced striatal dopamine**.
19. SNpc dopaminergic neurons lose their axons, causing **dopaminergic deafferentation of caudate and putamen**. About **50–70% of these neurons are gone before symptoms appear.**
20. **Lewy bodies** (α-synuclein-rich inclusions) are the hallmark and are thought to begin years before motor signs. **Over 90% of cases are sporadic**, with genetic predisposition, age and environmental toxins as risk factors.
21. Non-motor features: depression (with anxiety) precedes motor signs in about half; hallucinations, executive dysfunction, dementia, sleep and appetite change. Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated. Nothing yet slows progression.
22. **L-DOPA**, the dopamine precursor, is the main drug, but ~80% of patients develop motor fluctuations and dyskinesias within 5–8 years, attributed to continuing neuron loss plus intermittent drug availability. PET figure: receptor and transporter signal declines modestly with normal age; FDOPA uptake is markedly lower in PD.
23. **Gene therapy (LV/AAV):** TH + AADC into striatum gave ~30% motor improvement in rats and monkeys; a GABA-synthesis gene in glutamatergic subthalamic neurons was effective preclinically and safe in a unilateral phase I trial; **GDNF** gave "good results" by protein or vector delivery (the slide does not say in which species; context beyond the slides: controlled human trials of infused GDNF protein have not met their primary endpoints), AAV2-GDNF was safe in phase I, and a phase 2 with convection-enhanced delivery is underway; **neurturin** (AAV2-NRTN) was effective in rodents and primates but **safe without efficacy** in 38 patients at 1 and 2 years, and was discontinued.
24. **Cell therapy:** grafts of adrenal medulla, human or porcine fetal ventral mesencephalon, or retinal pigment epithelium aim to supply dopamine continuously. Human fetal grafts have survived with benefit up to 18 years, but ethics, supply, immunogenicity and surgical variability push toward ESC/iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons.
25. Also: deep brain stimulation, rTMS (motor and cognitive benefit in meta-analysis; mood and long-term effects uncertain), new drugs, music/dance, and nanocarriers for BBB delivery.

### Huntington's disease

26. Inherited **single-gene** disorder (gene isolated 1993), onset usually mid-30s–40s. (Context beyond the slides: autosomal-dominant CAG-repeat expansion in *HTT*.)
27. Involuntary movement, rigidity, impaired swallowing and speech, plus early subtle cognitive and psychiatric change. Neuron loss is concentrated in **basal ganglia and cortex**; death follows ~15–20 years after onset, usually from complications.
28. ~6/100,000 in Western countries and Australia; ~700/100,000 around Lake Maracaibo, Venezuela.
29. Treatment is symptomatic (tetrabenazine; antipsychotics). Huntingtin-lowering: AAV-delivered AMT-130 and antisense tominersen (failed, now re-trialled in milder patients).

### Multiple sclerosis

30. >2.8 million worldwide; onset 20–40; female:male ~3:1; more common at higher latitudes.
31. Immune damage to CNS myelin; symptoms depend on lesion site. Early lesions are packed with inflammatory cells; late lesions have few immune cells but extensive demyelination, astrogliosis and oligodendrocyte loss. Mostly white matter, but grey matter is affected too.
32. First episode = **clinically isolated syndrome**: optic nerve (blurred vision), spinal cord (motor/sensory loss), brainstem (vomiting, double vision).
33. **Courses:** relapsing–remitting (~85–90% at onset), secondary progressive, primary progressive (~10–15%), progressive-relapsing. In the pathomechanism figure, inflammation and repair wane while neurodegeneration rises with disability.
34. Damaged BBB → T cells release cytokines and recruit other immune cells → B cells produce demyelinating antibodies.
35. **Treatments are immunomodulatory:** corticosteroids shorten attacks; interferon-β is anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative; glatiramer acetate (mechanism uncertain); **natalizumab** blocks immune-cell migration across the BBB.
36. **Risk:** latitude, UV/vitamin D, smoking, affected siblings, **HLA-DRB1*15:01** (~3-fold). **Pathogens and diet (associations):** >99% of patients have had EBV, but so have most adults without MS, so that figure alone cannot show cause (context beyond the slides: prospective cohort data now strongly implicate EBV as a leading, though not sufficient, cause); a chlamydial hypothesis treated with long antibiotic courses (the slide flags it as receiving scientific criticism); coffee (non-drinkers ~1.5× risk vs 4 cups/day, with the slide's own coffee-vs-caffeine caution).

### Stroke

37. Deficits depend on vascular territory, severity and **haemorrhage vs clot**. Endogenous growth factors (BDNF) are neuroprotective; candidates include poly-arginine peptides R18/R18D, which improve functional outcome after endothelin-1-induced stroke (a vasoconstrictor animal model — context beyond the slides), and APP analogues.

### Injury and repair

38. **PNS vs CNS (figure):** in PNS, macrophages clear myelin debris quickly, the neuron expresses growth genes, and proliferating Schwann cells promote regrowth. In CNS, myelin debris lingers and astrocyte-associated inhibitory factors block extension.
39. **Reactions to axon injury (figure):** terminal and Wallerian degeneration, myelin debris, microglia/macrophage infiltration, chromatolysis, retraction of synaptic terminals, and transneuronal degeneration in both directions.
40. **PNS neurosurgery (figures):** proximal end-bulb and dieback, distal degeneration, scar, misdirected regrowth and fascicular atrophy. Repairs: epineural, fascicular, conduit (tubulisation), nerve graft.
41. **Traumatic injury:** primary = mechanical disruption, necrosis, haemorrhage; secondary = inflammation, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, demyelination, cysts, glial scarring.
42. Reactive astrocytes first **seal off the lesion**; the next goal is keeping spared cells healthy. A time-course figure in this astrocyte context shows NGF and bFGF mRNA rising within hours of an oxidative (H₂O₂) challenge — a rapid trophic response.
43. **Repair toolkit:** pharmacological (methylprednisolone, riluzole, Rho/ROCK blockers, anti-Nogo, minocycline), non-invasive (red/near-infrared light, magnetic stimulation), growth factors and viral gene delivery (NT-3, BDNF, GDNF, CNTF), scaffolds, cell transplants (olfactory ensheathing glia, Schwann cells, nerve grafts, stem/progenitor cells, iPSCs), surgery, combinations — plus deployed devices (DBS, cochlear and retinal implants, functional electrical stimulation, prosthetic limbs).

## Causal mechanism chains

```text
PARKINSON'S THRESHOLD
alpha-synuclein / Lewy pathology (years before symptoms)
-> loss of SNpc dopaminergic neurons and their striatal axons
-> dopaminergic deafferentation of caudate + putamen
-> striatal dopamine falls; symptoms only once ~50-70% of neurons are lost
-> tremor, rigidity, akinesia, postural instability
-> oral L-DOPA restores dopamine, but ongoing loss + intermittent dosing
   -> fluctuating availability -> motor fluctuations and dyskinesias
```

```text
MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS
BBB damage -> T cells enter, release cytokines, recruit immune cells
-> B cells produce demyelinating antibodies
-> demyelination impairs conduction -> symptoms set by lesion site
-> early: inflammation + repair dominate (relapse, remission)
-> late: inflammation wanes, neurodegeneration drives progressive disability
```

```text
TRAUMATIC CNS INJURY
PRIMARY: mechanical disruption, necrosis, haemorrhage (fixed at impact)
-> SECONDARY: inflammation, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage
-> demyelination, cysts, transneuronal degeneration
-> reactive astrocytes seal the lesion (protective) -> scar + matrix (inhibitory)
-> slow debris clearance + inhibitory factors -> regeneration suppressed
```

```text
EXERCISE AND TROPHIC SUPPORT
running -> hippocampal BDNF up (rodent; scales with distance)
-> survival + proliferation of new dentate neurons
-> more new cells integrate
-> human: walking/fitness associated with larger grey-matter/hippocampal volume
   (BDNF mediation possible, not demonstrated)
```

## Key experiments

| Experiment | Design | Result | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rodent wheel running | Exercised vs sedentary rodents; hippocampal BDNF and labelled new dentate cells | Higher BDNF, scaling with distance; more proliferating and surviving new cells | Exercise causally raises trophic support and neurogenesis — **in rodents** |
| Walking and grey matter | Older adults' walking recorded, MRI ~9 years later | More walking, more prefrontal/temporal/hippocampal grey matter, plateauing at moderate distance | **Association** in humans; BDNF mediation is a hypothesis |
| Spine resilience (Boros et al. 2017; design from the paper, context beyond the slides) | Post-mortem prefrontal spines in controls, cognitively normal people with AD pathology, and AD dementia | Spines preserved in the pathology-without-dementia group, reduced in AD | Spine plasticity is a candidate resilience mechanism; cross-sectional, not causal |
| AAV2-neurturin translation | Trophic gene therapy effective in rodent and primate PD models, then tested in 38 patients | Safe, but no efficacy at 1 or 2 years; discontinued | Animal efficacy does not guarantee human efficacy |

## Examiner traps / common overclaims

- **"Ageing = steady neuron loss everywhere."** Loss is region-selective (thalamus largely spared); synaptic simplification, blood flow and glial change matter as much.
- **"Plaques = Alzheimer's."** Plaques and tangles occur in normal ageing, and plaque load need not track dementia.
- **"Exercise works through BDNF."** In rodents, running experimentally raises both BDNF and neurogenesis, but the slide's figures show the two rising together rather than testing whether BDNF is required; in humans the grey-matter link is correlational and BDNF mediation is only possible.
- **"Symptoms mark onset."** In PD, Lewy pathology and 50–70% neuron loss precede motor signs; depression often comes first.
- **"Worked in monkeys, so it works in patients."** Neurturin was safe but ineffective in humans; mouse MSC results in AD are animal evidence.
- **"Any immunotherapy slows all MS."** Anti-inflammatory drugs target the inflammatory phase; progressive disability tracks neurodegeneration.
- **"Near-universal EBV exposure (or a coffee association) proves cause."** Prevalence among patients and observational diet links are associations; the slide flags criticism of the chlamydial hypothesis and a coffee-vs-caffeine question. (Context beyond the slides: the causal case for EBV rests on prospective cohort data, not on the >99% figure.)
- **"CNS neurons simply cannot regrow."** The figure blames the environment — slow debris clearance and inhibitory glial factors — against the Schwann-cell-supported PNS.

## Interrogation prompts

1. A neuroprotective drug halts all further SNpc loss from the day of PD diagnosis. Predict the effect on existing motor signs and on the need for L-DOPA.
2. L-DOPA is given as a continuous infusion rather than oral doses. Predict the effect on dyskinesias, using the slide's two-factor explanation.
3. AAV2-GDNF is delivered to a patient with modest versus near-total nigral loss. In which patient should a trophic factor work better, and why?
4. Natalizumab is tested in relapsing–remitting versus primary progressive MS. Predict which group benefits more.
5. A neuroprotectant targeting excitotoxicity is given one hour versus one week after spinal cord injury. Which events can each still modify?
6. The slide ties memantine's target to secondary damage in both disease and injury. What shared mechanism links the two, and what would blocking it fail to fix in AD?
7. FGF-2 is given to aged dentate gyrus where FGFR1 is reduced. Predict whether the response matches that in young tissue.
8. A brain at autopsy carries heavy plaque load, but the person was cognitively normal. What hypothesis does this support, and what can't it show?
9. Design a study separating a coffee effect from a caffeine effect on MS risk, and name one source of reverse causation.
10. A transected peripheral nerve is repaired with a conduit; the same is tried in the spinal cord. Predict both outcomes and name the cells responsible.
