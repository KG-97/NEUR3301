## What the lecture is really about

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is framed as a **two-stage injury**. A mechanical primary insult is over in moments. A **biological secondary cascade** then keeps enlarging the lesion for days to weeks. Nearly every therapy shown tries to do one of three things: shrink that secondary wave, rebuild or reconnect what was lost, or bypass the lesion. The second half of the lecture is about translation. Rodent models, rat rating scales and acute-phase wins do not map neatly onto chronic human injury.

The learning-outcomes slide asks you to **illustrate your understanding with examples**, so back each claim with a named strategy or experiment. The UWA studies of human mesenchymal precursor cells (hMPCs) and AAV-CNTF are the lecturer's own worked examples.

## What you need to know cold

### Scale, anatomy and classification

1. **Who it affects.** Mostly young adults (the majority are roughly 15–30), and about 61% are male. Australia sees about 400 new cases a year, mainly from road trauma and sport or other causes. In the USA, violence adds a large share. Lifetime cost rises steeply with injury level: a high cervical injury needing a ventilator costs about twenty times as much as thoracic paraplegia. Learn the pattern, not every number.
2. **Anatomy.** White matter holds the ascending and descending tracts; grey matter holds the cell bodies. The dorsal half carries the sensory roots and ganglia and the ventral half the motor roots. The two roots join laterally to form the spinal nerves.
3. **Complete vs incomplete.** In a complete injury, every ascending and descending tract is cut and all function below the level is lost. This is **very rare in humans**. In an incomplete injury, a small fraction of pathways survives. Those spared fibres are what recovery, plasticity and most therapies work with.
4. **"Acute" and "chronic" mean different times in different species.** In humans, acute is about the first 1–3 days, chronic is about a year on, and subacute lies in between. In animal models, acute means hours to days and chronic means weeks to months. The pathology moves from haemorrhagic necrosis and oedema, through a phase of limited repair and reorganisation, to central cysts in shrunken tissue walled off by glial scar.

### Biphasic pathophysiology

5. **Primary injury** is usually a contusion. Bone, disc fragments, haematoma and swelling compress the cord inside a closed canal. The dura is usually intact and the cord is usually **not** cut through. Haemorrhage leaves a core of dead tissue (the necrotic epicentre).
6. **Secondary injury** spreads outward and along the cord: **millimetres in rodents, centimetres in humans**. Grey matter is devastated and cysts form, while a rim of outer white matter is often spared.
7. In damaged white matter, axons degenerate and **progressively lose myelin as oligodendrocytes die**. Their targets lose input and the axon stump nearest the cell body retracts.
8. The secondary events to list are the inflammatory cascade, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, demyelination, cyst formation and glial scarring.

### Excitotoxicity and oxidative damage

9. Injury raises glutamate outside cells. **NMDA receptors** let Ca²⁺ in, which activates calmodulin, kinases and **calpain** and leads to apoptosis. **AMPA receptors** let Na⁺ in, which causes osmotic swelling and necrosis. Ca²⁺ overload in mitochondria generates **reactive oxygen species (ROS)**, which break down membrane lipids into toxic products (lipid peroxidation).
10. ROS are normal signalling molecules. Enzymes such as **superoxide dismutase** neutralise them until they are overwhelmed. **Nitric oxide** (made by immune cells, and also a vasodilator) combines with superoxide to form **peroxynitrite** (spelled "peroxynitrate" on the slide), which adds further damage.
11. **Oligodendrocytes undergo apoptosis more readily than neurons.** An axon that survived the impact can still fail because it loses its myelin.
12. **Reactive astrocytes** activate immediately and lay down matrix, including chondroitin sulphate proteoglycans (**CSPGs**). This forms a scar around the cysts that hinders regeneration and remyelination. Lecture 7 covers the scar's protective side.

### Immune and inflammatory response

13. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) are the first, innate wave of immune cells; lymphocytes come second. **TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6** promote damage, and TNF-α mRNA rises within an hour. Microglia release cytokines that kill oligodendrocytes, and they make ROS. Debris triggers phagocytosis and the release of neurotoxins, which hollow out tissue and strip myelin. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) add to the damage.
14. **Timeline** (mainly animal data; varies by species and strain):

| Cell | Arrives | Peak | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resident microglia | minutes–hours | ~24 h | by ~1 month–1 year most remaining cells are no longer phagocytic (M1→M2 shift) |
| Neutrophils | ~4 h | 24–72 h | stay elevated ~10 days |
| Macrophages | by day 2 | days 5–7 | persist weeks–months; M1-like destroy, M2-like repair |
| T cells | ~day 3 | — | role unclear; T cells that attack the body's own tissue worsen injury |

15. **Macrophages are double-edged.** They cause collateral (bystander) damage through cytokines, ROS, NO and proteases, but they also clear debris and release protective cytokines. In animals, macrophages pre-activated with pre-degenerated nerve before transplantation improved survival and regeneration. This result is contested, depends on timing, and may vary by species.

### Acute care and outcome measures

16. **Acute care:** a cervical collar and halo traction to immobilise the spine; cooling with cold saline is increasingly used; anti-TNF treatment and hyperbaric oxygen have been suggested. High-dose **methylprednisolone** within 8 h was widely used but is no longer routinely recommended because of infection risk and side effects.
17. **Human scales (reference only).** The **ASIA scale** runs from A to E:
    - A: complete (no function in sacral segments S4–S5).
    - B: incomplete for sensation only.
    - C: motor-incomplete, with most key muscles below grade 3.
    - D: motor-incomplete, with at least half the key muscles at grade 3 or more.
    - E: normal.

    It misses small changes, samples few spinal levels, and ignores pain and bowel, bladder and sexual function. Other measures are WISCI, FIM, SCIM, the Ashworth scale for spasticity, quantitative sensory testing, and motor- and sensory-evoked potentials (MEP/SSEP) as correlates of impairment.
18. **Animal tests:** the **BBB** open-field score (0–21, non-linear; 9 = weight support), Ratwalk/Catwalk gait analysis, the ladder walk (counts missteps), the rotarod, and forelimb tests for cervical injury.
19. **Species gap:** size, gait, anatomy, physiology and immunity all differ. Humans and rats form fluid-filled cysts, whereas mice fill the lesion with dense connective tissue, which probably contributes to differences in recovery. Non-human primates may help bridge the gap to the clinic.

### Problem → strategy

| Problem | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Damaged pathways | Regenerate, repair or replace |
| Demyelination | Remyelinate |
| Cysts, degeneration | Increase tissue sparing |
| Inhibitory scar | Reduce scar formation |
| Inflammation / oxidative stress | Modulate immunity; limit oxidative damage |

20. **Drug therapies.**
    - **Methylprednisolone** is a glucocorticoid: it raises anti-inflammatory cytokines and reduces lipid peroxidation.
    - **Riluzole** blocks Na⁺ channels and so cuts glutamate release. It helped in animals and was tested in a human trial (Fehlings 2023).
    - The **Rho–ROCK pathway** blocks axon regeneration. Cethrin/VX-210 inhibits it (the slide lists its phase 2b/3 trial as ongoing; context beyond the slides: that trial was stopped early for futility and missed its primary endpoint, Fehlings et al. 2021), anti-Nogo antibodies act on the same pathway, and Y-27632 is also listed.
    - **Rolipram** (a PDE4 inhibitor) and **minocycline** protect neurons.
    - **EphA4 blockade** reduces scarring, and **MMP blockade** targets matrix deposition.
    - (context beyond the slides) Nogo-A and other myelin-derived inhibitors signal through RhoA–ROCK to collapse growth cones. Inhibiting PDE4 raises cAMP, which favours growth. The riluzole trial (RISCIS) stopped early, was underpowered and missed its primary endpoint; only its secondary analyses suggested benefit.
21. **Non-drug therapies.** **Hypothermia**: cooling to 32–34 °C for 24–48 h, then slow rewarming. Its mechanism is unclear, but it lowers metabolism, inflammation, blood flow, free radicals and lactate. **Exosomes**: 30–150 nm vesicles carrying proteins, lipids, mRNA and microRNA. One microRNA, miR-126, promotes new blood vessel growth and reduces inflammation.
22. **Growth factors** (NT-3, BDNF, GDNF, NGF, bFGF) promote axon growth, rescue neurons whose axons were cut, and reduce shrinkage. They also **prolong the normally brief expression of regeneration-associated genes**. They can be given as protein into the lesion, by virus, in scaffolds, or carried by transplanted cells.
23. **Gene therapy.** AAV gives lasting local expression after one injection, with the protein made by the host's own cells. However, its spread, duration and dose are hard to control, and leakage into nearby tissue risks unwanted effects. Lentiviral vectors insert permanently into the genome of both dividing and non-dividing cells, and donor cells can be modified outside the body before grafting. The slides flag **switchable (on/off) expression** as the next essential step. One clinical programme uses AAV to convert spinal astrocytes into neurons.
24. **Tissue engineering.** Scaffolds should be compatible with tissue, grow blood vessels, and let axons grow through **and beyond** them. Success depends on surface charge, protein adsorption, stiffness, how fast the scaffold breaks down, longitudinal channels, and any toxic breakdown products. Newer options are thermosensitive hydrogels that set in place, injectable hydrogels that flow under pressure and then set, and electrospun nanofibres.
25. **Cell transplantation trade-offs:**

| Cell | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Embryonic stem cells | Pluripotent, unlimited supply | Teratoma, ethics, rejection |
| Neural stem/progenitor cells | Consistent gains; OPCs remyelinate | Few neurons; purity, tumour risk |
| Bone marrow stromal / mesenchymal precursors | Can come from the patient; consistent functional gains | Anatomical benefit unclear; mixed population; lasting benefit unproven |
| Foetal cord, peripheral nerve grafts | Anatomical and functional gains | Axons **fail to exit** without growth factors |
| Schwann cells | Best combined with OEGs | Only some tracts respond; strong astrocyte reaction |
| Olfactory ensheathing glia (OEGs) | Tissue sparing, neuroprotection, outgrowth | Results vary by source; effect on their own unclear |
| iPSCs | Can come from the patient; no embryo needed | Teratoma; reprogramming poorly understood |

### Translation

26. **Trials (reference only).** About 2,000 SCI trials were listed on clinicaltrials.gov in 2025; of the categories the slide counts, robotics (149) and TMS (141) are the largest, though together they are under a sixth of the total. Under 60 are cell-based, and about a dozen each use gene therapy or scaffolds. Trial guidelines stress careful patient selection, allowing for **spontaneous recovery**, and enough statistical power. An early embryonic stem cell trial (2010) was halted within a year. Grafts of the patient's own OEGs proved safe, and MSC trials are the most numerous. The patient's own macrophages and Schwann cells have passed early safety testing.
27. **Bypass strategies:** brain–computer and brain–spine interfaces, robotic rehabilitation and exoskeletons.
28. **Closing argument.** Most successes come in acute or subacute injury; evidence of benefit in chronic injury is scant. The lecture floats **"controlled re-injury"**: scraping away a chronic scar mechanically or digesting it with enzymes, to recreate an acute-like state where proven acute strategies might work. This is a proposal, not an established therapy.

## Causal chains

```text
Contusion inside closed canal (dura intact, cord not cut)
-> haemorrhage + necrotic epicentre
-> glutamate, ROS/NO, cytokines spread outward and along the cord (mm rodent, cm human)
-> grey-matter loss, oligodendrocyte apoptosis, demyelination of surviving axons
-> chronic cysts in shrunken tissue walled by CSPG-rich scar
```

```text
Excess glutamate
   -> NMDA receptor -> Ca2+ in -> calmodulin -> kinases + calpain -> APOPTOSIS
   -> AMPA receptor -> Na+ in -> osmotic swelling -> NECROSIS
   -> mitochondrial Ca2+ overload -> ROS -> lipid peroxidation
                                  -> ROS + NO -> peroxynitrite -> more damage
```

```text
Macrophages (day 2, peak days 5-7, persist weeks-months)
   -> early M1-like: TNF-a, IL-1, IL-6, ROS, NO, proteases -> bystander damage
   -> later M2-like: debris clearance + protective cytokines -> repair
=> blanket suppression trades less damage for less repair; timing decides
```

## Key experiments

**1. Human MPC grafts (UWA).**
- *Design:* hMPCs were taken from adult hip-bone (iliac-crest) marrow, purified and labelled. They were injected into immunodeficient (nude) rats after a moderate T10 contusion, either acutely (1 week) or chronically (the design slide says 2 months, but the tissue-sparing and BBB slides place the chronic graft about 1 month after injury). Controls received injury only, or injury plus culture medium.
- *Result:* In the dish, the cells were presented as promoting growth from dorsal root ganglion explants. In the rats, **no donor cells remained after one month**. Even so, more tissue was spared and cysts were smaller (the cyst reduction is marked significant only after acute grafts), and treated rats finished about 2–3 BBB points above controls (~15 vs ~12–13 after acute grafts; controls also passed the weight-support score of 9), with consistent plantar stepping and coordinated fore–hind limb movement. The slide calls acute and chronic outcomes similar, but in the graph the chronic-graft gain is smaller and significant only at the last two time points.
- *Inference:* The benefit outlasts the cells, so lasting cell replacement is unlikely to explain it. The slides list possible mechanisms: immune suppression, boosted endogenous repair, remyelination, and matrix deposition that offsets the scar. The mechanism is unresolved, and the evidence is from rats only.

**2. Cortical AAV-CNTF to the corticospinal tract (UWA).**
- *Design:* AAV carrying CNTF plus a fluorescent reporter was injected into motor cortex. Corticospinal tract (CST) neurons, which express CNTF receptor α, then make the factor themselves; the slides contrast this with placing CNTF in the lesion. BDA tracer was injected into the same cortex 2 weeks before tissue collection, and some rats also received rat MPCs.
- *Result:* By about 6 weeks, treated rats had better BBB scores, fewer ladder missteps and near-normal Ratwalk stepping. Labelled CST axons reached about 4 mm beyond the lesion, and cysts were smaller. CNTF plus viable MPCs increased tissue sparing.
- *Inference:* Transducing the parent neurons can enhance CST repair.
- (context beyond the slides) The 2018 paper gave the AAV two weeks **before** the contusion and the MPCs two weeks after it; it describes CST axons caudal to the lesion as only occasional and reports a significant cyst reduction only with CNTF plus viable MPCs. For the AAV timing, one slide instead says "just after". Check the timing before quoting it.

**3. Human iPSC-derived neural stem cells (Lu et al. 2014).**
- *Design:* The cells were grafted into injured rat spinal cord and examined about 3 months later.
- *Result:* Axons grew remarkably long distances above and below the lesion and formed synaptic structures, **but function did not improve**.
- *Inference:* Anatomical regeneration is not the same as recovery. The new connections must be appropriate and functional.

## Examiner traps

- **Treating human SCI as a clean transection.** Most are incomplete contusions with an intact dura.
- **Aiming drugs at the primary injury.** Drugs act on the secondary cascade.
- **"Inflammation is bad."** Phenotype and timing decide; macrophages also clear debris and support repair.
- **"More axons means recovery."** Lu 2014 shows growth without function; the hMPC study shows function without surviving graft cells.
- **Treating BBB or ASIA scores as linear.** Both are ranked (ordinal) scales with known blind spots.
- **Calling riluzole or methylprednisolone proven human treatments.**
- **Assuming acute rodent success transfers to chronic human injury.**
- **"Growth factors cause regeneration"**, without saying which factor, where, how, when and for how long.

## Interrogation prompts

1. Block only AMPA receptors at the lesion. Which mode of death falls, and which continues?
2. Give a drug that mimics superoxide dismutase. Predict the effect on peroxynitrite and oligodendrocyte survival.
3. Deplete infiltrating macrophages from day 2 to week 8. Predict the early and late consequences.
4. One rat's BBB rises from 7 to 11, another's from 15 to 19. Why are these not equivalent?
5. A graft improves BBB scores, but its cells are gone by 4 weeks. Design a test of whether secreted factors explain the effect.
6. AAV-CNTF is injected into the lesion instead of the cortex. What targeting is lost?
7. Why might a therapy that works after mouse contusion fail in humans?
8. Argue for and against "controlled re-injury" in chronic SCI.
