## What this lecture is really about

The deck follows one vesicle around a loop. The vesicle is made and filled, then parked in a reserve pool. It is recruited to the active zone, then tethered, docked and primed by SNAREs and their helpers. A calcium trigger fuses it with the membrane, and it is then retrieved and reused. Two questions frame the loop: **how do we know release happened** (measurement), and **how do specialised synapses tune release** (ribbon synapses).

The exam-safe stance is to keep three roles apart. **SNAREs are the fusion engine**, **synaptotagmin is the calcium trigger**, and **NSF/α-SNAP plus the endocytic proteins are the reset crew**. Most traps come from merging those roles, or from treating a measurement such as capacitance as if it were the mechanism.

## What you need to know cold

### Where release happens

1. The **active zone** is the release site, where vesicles and voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels are both densely packed. At the **neuromuscular junction (NMJ)**, active zones lie directly opposite clusters of postsynaptic ACh receptors, so Ca²⁺ entry, fusion and receptor binding line up. A CNS electron micrograph on the slide shows small clear vesicles crowded at the active zone, with a large dense-core vesicle further back.
2. CNS boutons are **much smaller** than the NMJ and have only **one or a few active zones**.

### How release is measured

3. **Membrane capacitance:** charge storage scales with membrane area. Fusion adds vesicle membrane to the surface and raises capacitance; retrieval lowers it. In the slide's textbook panels, mast-cell capacitance climbs in discrete steps during secretion and steps down during retrieval. In a neuronal panel, a brief depolarisation produces a Ca²⁺ current, a capacitance jump and a rise in internal Ca²⁺, and the capacitance then decays over seconds.
4. **Fluorescent dyes:** vesicles are preloaded with dye, and release is imaged as the dye leaves.
5. **Electrochemical probes:** these detect released chemical (the slide says "breakdown products"). Context beyond the slides: amperometry oxidises transmitters such as catecholamines directly.

### Vesicle cycle and pools

6. The cycle starts in the **soma**, and vesicle components are trafficked to the target terminal. The slide says vesicles are "made in the lysosome". Standard accounts place synthesis in the ER and Golgi, with local recycling via endosomes, and the cited figure shows an endosome.
7. In the terminal, vesicles are **filled with transmitter** and **tethered to the cytoskeleton**. A Ca²⁺ rise promotes docking. After exocytosis, the membrane is retrieved and recycled.
8. **Reserve (storage) pool:** vesicles held by **synapsin I** on the actin cytoskeleton. The slide writes "microtubules (actin)", but its figure shows actin filaments. These vesicles are recruited mainly at **higher stimulation frequencies**.
9. **Readily releasable pool (RRP):** docked, primed vesicles at the active zone, available immediately. They supply the quanta of ordinary transmission. The figure also shows an intermediate recycling pool.
10. **Recruitment from reserve pool to RRP:** Ca²⁺ entry during spikes activates protein kinases (the figure names **CaM kinase II**, with PKC also shown). The kinases **phosphorylate synapsin**, which frees vesicles from actin so they can move to the active zone.

### SNAREs: tethering, docking, priming, fusion

11. **Priming** prepares a docked vesicle to fuse. Tethering, docking and priming all involve the **SNARE complex**.
12. The **v-SNARE** on the vesicle is **synaptobrevin (VAMP)**. The **t-SNAREs** on the plasma membrane are **syntaxin and SNAP-25**. Together they form a four-helix core complex.
13. The helices **zip up** toward the membranes. **Trans-SNARE complexes** span both membranes and exert an inward force. After fusion, **cis-SNARE complexes** lie in one membrane and exert no force.
14. The slide proposes that membranes fuse once they are forced within about **1 nm**, and notes that the real process is more complicated. The cited figure shows the sequence: contact, hemifusion stalk, hemifusion diaphragm, initial pore, widened pore.
15. **Reset:** **α-SNAP and NSF** use **ATP** to pull cis-SNARE complexes apart so they can be reused.

### The trigger and the regulators

16. **Synaptotagmin** binds Ca²⁺, engages the partly zipped SNARE complex and drives the final zippering, which opens the pore.
17. Synaptotagmin has **C2A and C2B** domains. The slide assigns Ca²⁺ binding to C2A and **maximal association with syntaxin** to C2B. Mutations that block synaptotagmin (and other proteins) from binding syntaxin **reduce release**. Context beyond the slides: both C2 domains bind Ca²⁺, and the slide's own figure marks Ca²⁺ at both.
18. **Munc18** (n-Sec1) binds syntaxin and stabilises or opens it for docking and fusion. **Munc13** converts docked vesicles to a **primed** state by acting on the SNARE complex. Both sit **close to Ca²⁺ channels**, which allows rapid coupling. The active-zone figure also labels RIM, RIM-binding protein, Rab3/27 and **complexin**.
19. **Figure-only panels:**
    - The fusion model asks whether SNAREs alone can fuse membranes. It then shows Munc18-1 holding syntaxin closed until Munc13 and SNAP-25 allow assembly, with zippering then pulling the membranes together.
    - The cycle overview adds **chaperones** that keep the machinery working: CSPα–Hsc70–SGT at SNAP-25, and synuclein on the vesicle beside synaptobrevin.
20. The "complexity" slide lists synaptobrevin, synaptophysin, syntaxin, nSec1, SNAP-25, synaptotagmin, Rab3A, rabphilin-3A, NSF and α-SNAP. Know what each class does rather than every structure.

### Botulinum toxin

21. The toxin is endocytosed, and its **light chain is a protease that cleaves SNAREs**:
    - serotypes **B, D, F and G** cut synaptobrevin;
    - **A, C and E** cut SNAP-25;
    - **C** also cuts syntaxin.

    Without a complete SNARE complex, ACh is not released and the **muscle cannot contract**. The slide says this "kills the synapse forever". Context beyond the slides: clinical effects wear off over months as terminals recover, which is why injections are repeated.

### Retrieval and recycling

22. **Clathrin route (slow):**
    1. Adaptor proteins recruit **clathrin triskelia**, which form a curved coat.
    2. **Dynamin** rings pinch the neck.
    3. The coated vesicle moves on actin.
    4. **Hsc70 and auxilin** remove the coat.
    5. The vesicle is refilled.

    The cited figure gives this route τ ≈ **10–50 s**.
23. **Full-collapse fusion:** the vesicle flattens into the membrane and is retrieved by clathrin-dependent or clathrin-independent routes. The figure gives fast retrieval τ ≈ **1–10 s**.
24. **Kiss-and-run:** a transient pore opens and closes. Reuse is fast, but the vesicle **may not empty**. The cited figure marks this mode with a question mark because it is debated.
25. **Bulk endocytosis:** after heavy release, a large membrane infolding is taken up and small vesicles bud from it.

### Ribbon synapses and CNS variability

26. Cochlear hair-cell **ribbons tether many vesicles** beside Ca²⁺ channels, which supports **multiquantal**, high-speed release.
27. One hair cell contacts several afferent fibres through separate ribbons:
    - **low-threshold ribbons** may have **more Ca²⁺ channels** and release more at a given membrane potential;
    - **high-threshold ribbons** may have fewer channels and need more depolarisation.

    One receptor potential can therefore drive fibres that cover **very soft to very loud** sounds.
28. Alternatively, ribbons may differ in **fusion mode**, giving similar diversity in the size and shape of the postsynaptic response. The figure shows progressive, sequential, homotypic (vesicle–vesicle) and synchronised multivesicular fusion.
29. **CNS versus NMJ:** the fundamentals are shared, but CNS boutons have few active zones. Release mode (full fusion or kiss-and-run) and vesicle diameter vary, so **quantal size varies**. Multiquantal release also occurs.

## Quantitative corner: what a capacitance step means

The slides give the principle; the standard values below are context beyond the slides.

```text
C  = ε·A / d                (capacitance rises with area A, falls with thickness d)
cm ≈ 1 µF/cm²               (specific membrane capacitance)
ΔC = cm × A_added           (sphere: A = π·d²)
ΔC(t) = ΔC0 · e^(−t/τ)      (retrieval after a burst)
```

**Worked example.** Take a synaptic vesicle about 40 nm across.

- Its area is A = π × (4 × 10⁻⁶ cm)² ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹¹ cm², so ΔC ≈ 1 µF/cm² × 5 × 10⁻¹¹ cm² = 5 × 10⁻¹⁷ F, or **0.05 fF**.
- A 1 µm secretory granule adds about 31 fF, comparable to the steps in the slide's mast-cell trace (scale bar 25 fF). Single granules therefore show as steps, while single synaptic vesicles are lost in noise.
- A hypothetical 100 fF jump at a terminal implies about 100 / 0.05 ≈ **2,000 vesicles**.
- With slow retrieval (τ = 20 s), 37 fF of that jump remains at 20 s and about 5 fF at 60 s. Fast retrieval (τ = 2 s) is about 95% complete by 6 s.

Caveat: capacitance reports **net** area. Simultaneous exocytosis and endocytosis cancel, and the trace says nothing about how much transmitter left.

## Mechanism chains

```text
CALCIUM-TRIGGERED FAST RELEASE
Action potential invades terminal
-> active-zone Ca2+ channels open
-> local Ca2+ binds synaptotagmin C2 domains
-> final zippering of primed trans-SNARE complexes
-> hemifusion -> fusion pore -> transmitter released (capacitance rises)
```

```text
RESERVE POOL -> READILY RELEASABLE POOL
High-frequency firing raises terminal Ca2+
-> CaMKII (and PKC) activated -> synapsin phosphorylated
-> vesicles freed from actin -> move to active zone
-> tether -> dock -> Munc13/Munc18-assisted priming = RRP refilled
```

```text
SNARE ASSEMBLY AND RESET
Munc18 holds syntaxin
-> Munc13 + SNAP-25 allow assembly with synaptobrevin = trans-SNARE complex
-> zippering forces membranes to ~1 nm -> fusion
-> cis-SNARE complex (no force)
-> α-SNAP + NSF + ATP pull SNAREs apart -> reusable
```

```text
MEMBRANE RETRIEVAL AFTER FULL COLLAPSE
Vesicle membrane merges with surface
-> adaptors recruit clathrin -> coat curves membrane
-> dynamin pinches neck -> Hsc70 + auxilin uncoat
-> refill -> back to pools (capacitance falls, τ ~10-50 s)
```

## Key experiments

| Experiment | Design | Result | Inference and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacitance steps (Fernandez, Neher & Gomperts, 1984; rat mast cells) | Whole-cell capacitance recorded while GTPγS and ATP in the pipette trigger secretion | Capacitance rises in discrete steps, one per granule | Capacitance reads out the area added by exocytosis in real time. These cells secreted without Ca²⁺, so the steps show fusion, not its trigger. |
| Toxin cleavage of synaptobrevin (Schiavo et al., 1992; biochemistry and *Aplysia* neurons) | Tetanus and botulinum B light chains tested on vesicle proteins and injected with or without cleavage-site peptides | Zinc-protease cut of synaptobrevin-2 at one bond; release blocked, and decoy peptides delay the block | Intact synaptobrevin is **necessary** for release. This is loss-of-function evidence in invertebrate neurons. |
| Synaptotagmin I knockout (Geppert et al., 1994; mouse hippocampal culture) | Gene deleted; evoked, spontaneous and hypertonic-sucrose release recorded | Fast synchronous release strongly reduced; asynchronous, spontaneous and sucrose release intact | Synaptotagmin is needed for the **fast Ca²⁺ trigger**, not for fusion itself. A point mutation that lowers Ca²⁺ affinity halves the Ca²⁺ sensitivity of release (Fernández-Chacón et al., 2001), which ties Ca²⁺ binding to triggering. |
| Active-zone heterogeneity (Ohn et al., 2016; mouse inner hair cells) | Patch clamp plus Ca²⁺ imaging at single active zones within one cell | Half-activation voltage of Ca²⁺ influx spans about 20 mV. Zones with more channels faced the ganglion yet activated at **stronger** depolarisation | Supports diverse thresholds within one hair cell. However, threshold tracked the voltage dependence of Ca²⁺ influx, not channel number alone, so treat the slide's "may" literally. |

Human evidence here is essentially clinical: botulinum paralysis and therapy at the human NMJ. The mechanistic data come from rodents and invertebrates.

## Examiner traps and common overclaims

- **"Capacitance rises when a vesicle docks."** The slide is worded this way, but docking adds no area. Capacitance rises on **fusion**.
- **"Synaptotagmin fuses membranes."** It is the trigger; SNARE zippering supplies the force. Without synaptotagmin, slow and spontaneous fusion persist.
- **"NSF drives fusion."** NSF and α-SNAP **disassemble** spent cis-SNARE complexes after fusion.
- **"Botox blocks ACh receptors or Ca²⁺ channels."** It cleaves presynaptic SNAREs downstream of Ca²⁺ entry, and each serotype has its own target.
- **"Kiss-and-run is simply more efficient."** It allows faster reuse but may release only part of the contents, and how common it is remains debated.
- **"More Ca²⁺ channels always means a lower threshold."** The slide offers this as one possibility. Fusion mode and Ca²⁺-channel voltage dependence also matter.
- **Measurement versus mechanism.** Capacitance, dyes and probes show *that* release happened. Only perturbations such as toxins, knockouts and mutations show which proteins are **necessary**.

## Interrogation prompts

1. A dynamin inhibitor is applied during repetitive stimulation. Predict the capacitance trace and what happens to release over time.
2. Botulinum B is applied to one NMJ and botulinum A to another. Which SNARE is lost in each, and does ACh release differ?
3. Synapsin cannot be phosphorylated. Compare single-spike release with release late in a long high-frequency train.
4. NSF is inhibited. Fusion continues briefly and then fails. Explain why.
5. Synaptotagmin I is deleted. Predict how release is timed after a spike, and the frequency of minis.
6. Munc13 is deleted. Do vesicles still dock? Are they release-ready?
7. Some of a ribbon's Ca²⁺ channels are blocked. How does its afferent fibre's sound-level threshold change?
8. After a stimulus, capacitance recovers with τ ≈ 2 s instead of 20 s. Which mode is implicated, and what can capacitance not tell you?
