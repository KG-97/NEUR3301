## What you need to know cold

1. **Neurogenesis is five steps, not one.** Proliferation → migration → differentiation/maturation into adult identity → elaboration of dendrites and axons → **formation of a functional synapse and integration into the network**. Beros stressed the last step: making a neuron is not neurogenesis unless it integrates, and cells that fail to integrate are removed.
2. **Neurogenesis begins within the first month after conception**, is extensive through development, and declines — but does not stop — in adulthood. "The brain stops developing at 25" is a myth he named as such; the developmental period is roughly 25–30 years, and neurogenesis continues in specific regions after it.
3. **Potency hierarchy:** totipotent (any cell in the body, early embryonic) → pluripotent (most cell types; the ones used in embryonic stem-cell therapy) → multipotent (a *family* of cells — haematopoietic, mesenchymal) → oligopotent → unipotent (one cell type only).
4. **Three division outcomes**, and the distinction is exam-able:
   - **Symmetric self-renewal** — two stem daughters. *Expands* the pool.
   - **Asymmetric self-renewal** — one stem daughter, one differentiating daughter. *Maintains* the pool.
   - **Differentiative division** — both daughters differentiate. *Depletes* the pool.
5. **Neurogenic niche** = a small, specialised microenvironment that keeps stem cells in a stem-cell fate and permits controlled differentiation. In development: dorsal neural tube → PNS, ventral neural tube → CNS.
6. **Two adult neurogenic niches:** the **subventricular zone (SVZ)**, on the wall of the lateral ventricle, and the **subgranular zone (SGZ)**, in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus.
7. **SVZ → olfactory bulb via the rostral migratory stream (RMS).** Astrocyte-like **B cells** cling to blood vessels and form a tube; progenitors inside it drag themselves along each other by actin. Rodent migration distance ~2–5 mm — roughly 100× the cell's own length. Thousands of cells per day in rodent; not all survive. Limited cortical adult neurogenesis also occurs.
8. **Hippocampal anatomy for this lecture:** the trisynaptic circuit — entorhinal cortex → dentate gyrus → mossy fibres → CA3 → Schaffer collaterals → CA1. The dentate gyrus has a **granular layer** (cell bodies), a **molecular layer** (dendrites), and beneath the granular layer the **subgranular zone** where proliferation happens.
9. **The adult hippocampal lineage:** **Type 1** = radial glial-like cells (similar to but *not identical to* developmental radial glia) → **Type 2a** (least differentiated) → **Type 2b** → **neuroblast** → immature neuron → mature neuron. Some Type 1 cells become **astrocytes** instead.
10. **The product is the adult-born dentate granule cell (ABDGC).**
11. **Protracted maturation timeline (rodent):** precursor → immature neuron takes a few days to ~2 weeks; immature → mature takes about **6–8 weeks**. Human time course not established.
12. **Immature neurons are EXCITED by GABA for about the first 3 weeks.** They express a transporter that loads chloride in, so intracellular Cl⁻ is high; when GABA opens the channel, Cl⁻ flows **out**, and losing a negative ion depolarises the cell. As the neuron matures the transporter is downregulated and GABA becomes inhibitory as normal. This matters because the hippocampus is a strongly inhibitory environment — GABAergic excitation lets new cells participate.
13. Immature ABDGCs have **increased capacity for plasticity** and are implicated in **buffering the stress response**.
14. **Staging markers:** GFAP and SOX2 (stem/radial glial-like); **doublecortin (DCX)** switches on in immature neurons and off as they mature; **NeuN** marks mature neurons; Ki67 and ASCL1 mark proliferation.
15. **Adult-born cells are not identical to developmentally born ones.** Retrovirus + tdTomato labelling at postnatal day 1 versus in adulthood showed adult-born cells have larger nuclei at ~7 weeks, **greater dendritic spine density**, and **larger mossy-fibre boutons** — accounting for roughly 50% of dendritic size in the hippocampus. Functional consequences not yet established.

## Quantifying neurogenesis — the method table

This is the most MCQ-exposed part of the lecture, because each method has a specific blind spot.

| Method | What it does | The limitation Beros named |
|---|---|---|
| Post-mortem immunohistochemistry | Stain for stem/immature/mature markers | **Endpoint only.** You see the result, not *when* cells divided — so you cannot attribute a change to a specific intervention. |
| Retroviral tagging (e.g. tdTomato) | Retrovirus integrates only in **dividing** cells, so birth date is time-locked | Requires genetic modification. |
| **Tritiated thymidine** | Radioactive nucleoside incorporated into DNA during division; detected by autoradiography | Older method; radioactive. |
| **BrdU / EdU** | Non-radioactive thymidine analogue incorporated into dividing nuclei, detected by antibody | Dose matters — **mutagenic and teratogenic**, with cancer risk. |
| Two-photon live imaging | Cranial window over tdTomato-labelled stem cells; track the *same* cells across days | Invasive; requires skull/brain window. |
| **Human MRI** | Hippocampal volume, non-invasive | A snapshot of **volume**, not cell number — includes water and dendrites, and says nothing about cell type. |
| **Human ¹⁴C bomb-pulse dating** | Atmospheric ¹⁴C from nuclear testing dates when a cell's DNA was synthesised | Population-level birth dating. |

**The key conceptual point:** endpoint staining is correlative; only a method that *time-locks* division (BrdU, tritiated thymidine, retrovirus) can attribute new neurons to a specific window.

## What modulates adult neurogenesis

- **Neurotransmitters, stage-dependently.** Serotonin and norepinephrine **increase** proliferation. **GABA inhibits proliferation but then flips** — during the immature-to-mature transition it improves differentiation and survival. Glutamate matters for the mature functional synapse. The same transmitter has opposite effects at different stages; this is a designed MCQ trap.
- **Activity.** Epilepsy is associated with increased neurogenesis in some post-mortem studies.
- **Exercise and environmental enrichment** — the most replicable driver. Enriched cages with tubes, toys and running wheels increase hippocampal neurogenesis, raise **BDNF** and reduce inflammation. Increased neurogenesis correlates with better **Morris water maze** performance.
- **Social isolation / single housing** reduces it.
- **Stress reduces it.** In the social-defeat model, acute stress suppresses neurogenesis but recovers on return to normal housing with no lasting anxiety phenotype; **chronic** social defeat causes sustained suppression plus anxiety- and depression-like behaviour. **Prenatal maternal stress** reduces neurogenesis in offspring.
- **Drugs.** SSRIs such as fluoxetine increase neurogenesis; ECT and brain stimulation increase it.

## The human controversy — know both sides and the methodological critique

- **Eriksson et al. 1998** — the seminal positive study. Terminal cancer patients (n = 5, ages ~57–87) consented to **BrdU** injection; post-mortem hippocampus showed BrdU⁺ cells in the granular layer, some NeuN⁺ (neurons) and some not (likely astrocytes). This opened the field.
- **Sorrells et al. 2018 (Nature)** — the major negative study. Human foetal, child and adult post-mortem tissue plus primate work; reported that hippocampal neurogenesis **drops sharply in children to undetectable levels in adults** — no DCX, no evidence of new neurons after childhood. Highly influential and damaging to funding in the field.
- **Boldrini et al.**, published about a month later, **did** find adult human hippocampal neurogenesis.
- **The critiques of the negative result**, which are what you should be able to state:
  1. **Post-mortem delay** — tissue sometimes obtained up to 2 days after death, and DCX and immature-cell markers degrade quickly, so antibodies may fail to bind.
  2. **Over-fixation** — time in fixative was not reported, and it affects antibody binding.
  3. **Epileptic tissue used as control** — a poor comparison given epilepsy itself alters neurogenesis.
- **The field's current position:** most researchers still hold that adult human hippocampal neurogenesis occurs. Beros framed Sorrells as an important study with methodological questions, not as a bad one.

**Answer grammar for this topic:** absence of a marker under conditions that degrade that marker is not evidence of absence of the process.

2. Predict what happens to the stem-cell pool over time if divisions shift from asymmetric to differentiative. What would you measure to detect it?

4. Sorrells found no DCX in adult tissue. Give three methodological explanations that do not require adult human neurogenesis to be absent.
5. An animal is put in an enriched cage with a running wheel. Predict the change in neurogenesis, in BDNF, and in Morris water maze performance — and state why the correlation does not establish that new neurons caused the improvement.
6. Chronic social defeat versus a single episode. Predict the neurogenesis and behavioural outcome for each, and say what the difference implies about reversibility.
7. Why is hippocampal MRI volume a weak proxy for neurogenesis? Name two things included in the measurement that are not new neurons.
8. Serotonin increases proliferation but GABA inhibits it — yet GABA later promotes survival. Explain how one transmitter does both.
