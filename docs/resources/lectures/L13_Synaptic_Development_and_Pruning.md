## What this lecture is really about

Two halves, one idea. The first half asks how a synapse is **built**: at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), then in the CNS, where matched adhesion molecules do the job the synaptic basal lamina does in muscle. The second half asks how synapses are **removed**: NMJ elimination, the activity rules that pick winners, microglia, Hebbian refinement and ocular dominance columns.

The thread: pre- and postsynaptic sides build each other through **two-way signalling**, and that same two-way signalling, read through **relative, patterned activity**, decides which inputs survive. Nearly all causal evidence comes from frog and mouse preparations and from animal deprivation studies; the only human data are post-mortem synapse counts.

## What you need to know cold

### The developmental arc

1. Formation is a sequence (Südhof scheme): axons and dendrites grow (activity-independent) → contact is initiated → presynaptic organisation (a standard release machine) and postsynaptic specialisation (diverse receptors) specify each other → release and receptor machinery are tuned throughout life, often activity-dependently → physiological elimination, mostly around puberty but continuing. Dysfunction can tip this into pathological elimination.
2. Basic steps (panels A–F): a guided growth cone contacts its target, adhesion molecules stabilise the contact, then the terminal gathers vesicles while the target gathers receptors and scaffolds.

### The NMJ as the model synapse

3. Anatomy: a Schwann-cell-capped motor terminal, vesicles at active zones, basal lamina in the cleft, and AChR-lined junctional folds.
4. **The basal lamina carries instructions.** It is extracellular matrix (collagen plus proteins), rich in acetylcholinesterase at the synapse. Laminin is made by the muscle, and synaptic laminin (s-laminin; the β2 chain, context beyond the slides) is a **retrograde organiser** of the nerve terminal.
5. **Laminin mutants (mouse):** α2-deficient (*dy*) junctions are poorly formed, with fewer folds and receptors and Schwann-cell processes intruding into the cleft. β2 knockouts are worse: the Schwann cell covers the terminal, there is no vesicle release or receptor specialisation, and the animals die during development.
6. **Each side can assemble parts alone.** Uninnervated myotubes form AChR clusters, and an isolated growth cone releases ACh (detected by a receptor-rich membrane patch) before contact. Innervation then gathers the diffuse embryonic receptors into a dense patch under the terminal: the nerve organises components rather than creating them.

### Concentrating AChRs: four mechanisms

7. (i) **Redistribution and anchoring** in the membrane plane; (ii) **metabolic stabilisation**, with residence time rising from about 1 day (embryonic myotubes) to about 14 days (adult); (iii) **more transcription in synaptic nuclei**; (iv) **repression of transcription in extrasynaptic nuclei**.
8. **Agrin = anchoring.** Nerve-derived agrin, held in the basal lamina, activates the **MuSK** receptor complex (the figure also shows Lrp4 and Dok-7); MuSK is tyrosine-phosphorylated and **rapsyn** links AChRs into clusters tied to the cytoskeleton.
9. **Neuregulin = transcription.** Secreted by the nerve, it acts on **ErbB** receptors in the muscle membrane (and on Schwann cells); kinase cascades (Ras/ERK and Rac/JNK in the figure) raise AChR gene expression in the nuclei under the terminal. The slide adds that neuregulin matters for later plasticity too.
10. **Activity represses extrasynaptic AChRs.** Paralysis or denervation raises AChR transcription along the whole fibre; reinnervation reverses it; denervation plus **direct electrical stimulation of the muscle** prevents it. The repressor is the muscle's own electrical activity, not transmitter.

### The CNS: same logic, different parts

11. Shared features: central synapses are also pruned and also cluster receptors, which is harder because one neuron receives many inputs using different transmitters. Agrin acts centrally too (synapsin I aggregation presynaptically, receptor–scaffold aggregation postsynaptically).
12. Key difference: **no basal lamina in the central cleft**, so no synaptic laminin (brain laminin exists, made by some glia, endothelial cells and pericytes, for the BBB and meninges). Organisation relies on **matched pre/postsynaptic adhesion molecules**.
13. Sequence: (proto)cadherins mediate recognition → vesicles accumulate at the contact → inductive molecules (SynCAM, ephrinB/EphB, neurexin, neuroligin, neuregulin) are recruited to anchor the junction.
14. **Postsynaptic neuroligin sets synapse type.** Presynaptic neurexins bind **neuroligin 1**, which binds **PSD-95** to cluster **AMPA and NMDA receptors** (excitatory), or **neuroligin 2**, which binds **gephyrin** to cluster **GABA-A receptors** (inhibitory). The release machinery is largely shared; the vesicular transporter differs (VGLUT vs VGAT).

### Overproduction, then pruning

15. **Human synapse density** (post-mortem, layer 3 of middle frontal gyrus) rises after birth to an early-childhood peak, falls into adolescence, plateaus through adulthood and dips in old age. Elimination is framed as an **active** process.
16. Why overproduce? Hypotheses only: too few genes to specify every connection (experience fine-tunes), guaranteeing every muscle fibre is innervated, or a side effect of over-eager motor axons.

### Elimination at the NMJ

17. Immature muscle fibres receive **several motor axons** (polyneuronal innervation); mature fibres receive **one**. (The slide words this as 'each motor nerve innervates multiple muscle fibres' becoming 'one motor nerve innervates one muscle fibre'; read both as describing how many axons converge on each fibre, as the Lichtman & Colman figure shows.) Each motor neuron still supplies many fibres, so refinement is per fibre, and it is **selective, not random**.
18. Measured by stimulating the motor nerve and reading the muscle response (context beyond the slides: graded stimulation recruits each input as a separate step). In mouse, multiply innervated junctions fall from about 100% near birth to near zero by about P14.
19. **Losing-input sequence:** inputs **segregate** within the endplate → the winner **invades** as the loser **retracts**, nearest neighbours first → **AChRs are lost** under the loser → the axon thins and ends in a **retraction bulb**. The loser's **quantal content and strength fall** throughout.
20. **Receptor loss mostly comes first**, and focal receptor blockade mimics the process: receptors disappear from the blocked patch, then the overlying branch withdraws.
21. **Competition, not random loss:** fibres are never left uninnervated; early partial denervation leaves survivors with more terminals than usual; reinnervation repeats overproduction then elimination.
22. **Proposed effectors** of final retraction: "synaptotoxins" such as proteases; protease inhibitors given during development reduce elimination.

### How activity decides

23. **The postsynaptic cell referees.** Active terminals generate a local **maintenance (protection) signal** and a **punishment signal** that spreads to nearby sites, reaching the closest terminal first. An inactive terminal beside active ones loses; if **all** inputs are inactive, no punishment arises and all are kept. **Synchronous** firing shares protection and can preserve polyneuronal innervation; **differences** in firing between neighbours drive remodelling.
24. **JAK2:** the punishment signal activates **JAK2** (and its substrate **STAT1**) in the inactive synapse, which is then eliminated; with every input inactive there is no competition and no elimination. JAK2-driven recruitment of microglia is labelled a **proposal**. (Context beyond the slides: shown for callosal inputs in mouse cingulate cortex.)
25. **Microglia–synapse interactions**, four proposed modes: **destabilisation** (removing extracellular matrix, regulating excitability); **debris clean-up** (fragments, apoptotic bodies, axosomes); **modification**, by non-phagocytic **stripping** or by **trogocytosis** (nibbling small membrane pieces); and **engulfment** of membrane compartments.

### Hebbian refinement and ocular dominance

26. Correlated pre- and postsynaptic activity strengthens a synapse; reduced or uncorrelated activity weakens and removes it. Slide ingredients: **NMDA receptors and Ca²⁺ influx**, **competition**, **synchrony**, and **postsynaptic neurotrophic feedback** captured by active terminals.
27. **Ocular dominance columns:** geniculate afferents from each eye first overlap in layer 4 of V1, then segregate into alternating eye-specific bands with activity during the critical period. (Context beyond the slides: the slide's own source, Katz & Crowley 2002, reports that in cats, ferrets and monkeys columns emerge earlier and more precisely than this classic account, before the critical period, which is when experience can reshape them.)
28. **Monocular occlusion** in the critical period expands the open eye's territory; occlusion from birth leaves cortex unresponsive to that eye, a permanent cortical (not retinal) blindness for that eye. Earlier closure gives a larger shift (figure: closure at birth vs 2, 3 and 6 weeks). **Binocular deprivation** or **balanced activity** leaves cells driven by both eyes; **synchronous stimulation of both eyes** prevents columns forming. Adult manipulations have minor effects.

## Causal mechanism chains

```text
NMJ POSTSYNAPTIC ASSEMBLY
Motor axon arrives -> agrin deposited in synaptic basal lamina
-> agrin activates MuSK complex -> MuSK tyrosine phosphorylation
-> rapsyn links AChRs into cytoskeleton-anchored clusters
-> neuregulin -> ErbB -> more AChR transcription in synaptic nuclei
-> muscle action potentials repress extrasynaptic AChR genes
-> dense, stable cluster (residence ~1 day -> ~14 days)
```

```text
CNS SYNAPSE ASSEMBLY
(Proto)cadherin recognition -> vesicles accumulate at contact
-> neurexin binds neuroligin across the cleft
   -> NL1 + PSD-95  -> AMPAR/NMDAR cluster  = excitatory
   -> NL2 + gephyrin -> GABA-A R cluster    = inhibitory
```

```text
COMPETITIVE ELIMINATION
Inputs to one target fire out of sync
-> active input drives postsynaptic receptors
-> protection under active terminal + punishment spreading to neighbour
-> JAK2 -> STAT1 in the inactive terminal
-> receptors lost under loser -> quantal content falls -> territory shrinks
-> axon thins -> retraction bulb -> single innervation
   all inputs silent -> no punishment -> no elimination
```

```text
HEBBIAN SEGREGATION OF EYE INPUTS
Same-eye afferents fire together -> coincident pre + post depolarisation
-> NMDAR Ca2+ influx -> strengthening + postsynaptic trophic feedback
-> correlated terminals capture support and expand
-> other-eye inputs, uncorrelated with them, weaken and withdraw -> bands
```

## Worked numbers

**Receptor turnover.** Treat removal as first-order with mean residence time τ (context beyond the slides: a simplifying assumption), so N(t)/N₀ = e^(−t/τ). After one day, embryonic receptors (τ ≈ 1 day) keep e^(−1) ≈ 37%; adult receptors (τ ≈ 14 days) keep e^(−1/14) ≈ 93%. If the slide's figures are instead half-lives (the way AChR turnover is often reported), the one-day values become 50% and 2^(−1/14) ≈ 95%. At steady state, insertion equals N/τ, so a mature cluster of the same size needs about one-fourteenth of the insertion rate.

**Synapse density.** Huttenlocher's adult mean is about 11 × 10⁸ synapses/mm³, and the paper puts the age 1–2 peak about 50% higher (≈16.6 × 10⁸; the slide graph tops out near 17). Loss from peak = (16.6 − 11)/16.6 ≈ 33%. This is a *density*, so changes in tissue volume and neuron density also feed into it.

## Key experiments

| Experiment | Design | Result | Inference and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basal-lamina sheaths (Glicksman & Sanes 1983, frog) | Cut the nerve and damage the muscle so fibres degenerate but their basal lamina survives | Regenerating axons stop at old synaptic sites and form terminals with vesicle clusters, though not fully normal | Presynaptic cues sit in the synaptic basal lamina; no postsynaptic cell is needed. |
| Laminin mutants (mouse) | Delete or disable α2 or β2 laminin chains | α2: poor folds, Schwann processes in cleft. β2: Schwann cell caps terminal, no release, lethal | Muscle laminin is a retrograde organiser. Global mutants mix muscle and Schwann-cell effects. |
| Focal receptor blockade (slide figure; Balice-Gordon & Lichtman 1994, adult mouse in vivo) | Block AChRs in a small patch of one junction | Receptors lost there, then the overlying branch withdraws | Local inactivity triggers receptor loss, then retraction, even at a mature junction. (Context beyond the slides: whole-junction blockade causes no elimination.) |
| JAK2 (Yasuda et al. 2021, mouse) | Silence a subset of cortical neurons; manipulate JAK2/STAT1 | Silenced inputs eliminated via competition, JAK2-dependently; all-inactive inputs kept | Molecular transducer of punishment in the loser. Microglial link is a proposal. |
| Visual deprivation (animal; species not named on the slides) | Close one eye, both eyes, or stimulate synchronously, in or after the critical period | Open eye takes over; binocular closure keeps cells binocular; synchrony prevents columns; adult effects minor | Relative correlated activity, within a window, decides. |
| Synapse counts (Huttenlocher 1979, human) | Post-mortem electron microscopy, 21 brains, newborn to 90 years | Infant peak, decline to adolescence, adult plateau, late dip | Cross-sectional and correlational; density, not total number; no mechanism. |

## Examiner traps and common overclaims

- **"Mature NMJ: one motor neuron, one fibre."** Each fibre ends with one axon; each motor neuron still innervates many fibres.
- **"Inactive synapses are eliminated."** Only when active competitors are nearby. All-silent inputs are kept (shared inactivity, the JAK2 all-inactive condition, binocular deprivation).
- **"Agrin switches on AChR genes."** On the slides, agrin–MuSK–rapsyn anchors, neuregulin–ErbB drives synaptic transcription, and activity represses extrasynaptic transcription.
- **"Nerve-released ACh suppresses extrasynaptic receptors."** Direct muscle stimulation after denervation is enough.
- **"The axon retracts and the receptors follow."** Receptor loss mostly comes first.
- **"Laminin organises CNS synapses too."** The central cleft has no basal lamina; adhesion molecules do the job.
- **"Microglia prune by eating whole synapses."** There are four modes, including non-phagocytic stripping and trogocytosis, and the JAK2–microglia link is proposed.
- **"Synapse counts prove pruning matures cognition."** The human data are cross-sectional density measurements, not a mechanism.

## Interrogation prompts

1. A muscle is denervated and then stimulated directly. Predict extrasynaptic AChR levels and what this reveals about the repressing signal.
2. Agrin signalling is blocked but neuregulin is intact. Predict receptor clustering versus AChR transcription in synaptic nuclei.
3. α-bungarotoxin is applied to one third of a neonatal endplate. Predict the sequence at that site, then predict the result if the whole endplate is blocked.
4. Two motor neurons sharing the same fibres are forced to fire in synchrony through the first two postnatal weeks. Predict innervation at P14.
5. JAK2 is deleted from silenced neurons whose neighbours remain active. Predict synapse survival.
6. One eye is closed at birth, at 6 weeks, or in adulthood. Rank the shifts, then predict closing both eyes.
7. An NMDA receptor antagonist is infused into V1 during monocular deprivation. Using the slide's Hebbian ingredients, predict the effect on the shift.
